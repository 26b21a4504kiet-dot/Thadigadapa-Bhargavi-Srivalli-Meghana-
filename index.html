Musical Instrument Frequency Analyzer

A guitar string modelled as masses joined by springs. The eigenvalues of its stiffness matrix are the squared frequencies, and the eigenvectors are the shapes the string vibrates in.

Set up the string

Pick a benchmark guitar string or enter your own values. Everything below recalculates instantly.

Step 1: The stiffness matrix

Newton's law for each mass gives m·xᵢ″ = k(xᵢ₊₁ − 2xᵢ + xᵢ₋₁). Collecting all N equations gives x″ = −(k/m)·K·x, where K is tridiagonal with 2 on the diagonal and −1 beside it.








Step 2: Eigenvalues and frequencies


Trying x = v·cos(ωt) turns the motion into Kv = λv, with ω² = (k/m)·λ and f = ω / 2π. The highlighted rows are the first three harmonics.




Error in cents compares each mode with the ideal continuous string, f = j·f₁. 100 cents is one semitone.







Step 3: The first three vibration modes


Each eigenvector gives the relative displacement of every mass. Dots are the N-mass model; the dashed line is a continuous string.

















Frequency spectrum


Eigenvalue result against the ideal harmonics. High modes sag below the ideal because the beads cannot follow short wavelengths. Raise N to close the gap.

Assumptions


    

  1. N equal masses m joined by N + 1 identical massless springs of stiffness k, both ends fixed.
    2. 

  2. Small transverse vibrations, so Hooke's law holds, with no damping.
    3. 

  3. A string of length L, tension T and density μ maps to spacing a = L/(N+1), m = μa and k = T/a.
    4. 

  4. Tension comes from the ideal-string relation T = μ(2Lf₁)².
    5. 

  5. Eigenpairs are found numerically with the Jacobi rotation method. The closed form λⱼ = 2 − 2cos(jπ/(N+1)) is used only to verify them.
